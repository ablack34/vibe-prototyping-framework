## Persona 1: Marek Zawadzki _(Primary)_

| Field | Value |

| **Fictional name** | Marek Zawadzki |
| **Role / Key characteristic** | Senior Dispatcher, Warsaw Control Centre — institutional memory made human |
| **High-level description** | Marek has spent 12+ years dispatching field technicians across 14 countries. He holds the entire technician roster, SLA ruleset, and most reliable rerouting patterns in his head. He's effective and reliable — but that's part of the problem: the operation depends on him in ways that don't scale and are impossible to hand off to junior colleagues. |

### Key needs

- **Ranked technician recommendation with visible reasoning** — the system should surface the best option and explain why (certifications, proximity, job history at site) so Marek can confirm or override in one action. _[questionnaire-responses.md — Bartosz Nowak: "Tell me which tech should do the next order… here's why… a button to confirm."]_
- **Ranked backup options on refusal or unavailability** — if the first-choice tech is in traffic or says no, Marek needs instant second and third options without re-running his mental calculation. _[questionnaire-responses.md — "If the tech is busy or refuses, the system gives me a ranked second and third choice instantly."]_
- **Drive-time visibility in-app** — technician travel time shown in the dispatch UI so he never has to alt-tab to Google Maps. _[transcript-kickoff.md line 88–90]_
- **Proximity clustering** — the system should flag when two open orders are within walking distance of each other so they can be bundled into one trip. _[questionnaire-responses.md — "it can't see that two work orders are 800m apart and should be done by the same tech in one trip"]_
- **Proactive SLA risk flag, 90+ minutes before breach** — enough advance notice to call the customer before the breach rather than after. _[transcript-kickoff.md lines 44–48; customer-brief.md]_

### Key pains

- **Quadruple data re-entry in the 2008 system** — the same information must be entered four separate times per assignment, burning time and creating error risk. _[questionnaire-responses.md — "The system makes me re-enter the same information 4 times."]_
- **No proactive risk signal — surprises hurt most** — Marek only discovers a likely breach when a customer calls, by which point it is too late to prevent or soften it. _[transcript-kickoff.md lines 44–48]_
- **Institutional knowledge at scale breaks down** — when Marek is on leave, junior dispatchers make defensible-but-suboptimal calls that cost the business money hundreds of times a week. _[questionnaire-responses.md — Sandra: "the same handful of senior dispatchers know what to do but can't be in 14 timezones at once"]_
- **Workload is unsustainable** — three senior dispatchers have left in the past 18 months. Marek is explicit about the burnout risk. _[questionnaire-responses.md — Bartosz: "I will probably leave. We've lost 3 senior dispatchers in 18 months and the workload is brutal."]_
- **Fear of replacement, not just poor tools** — the 2019 Dispatcher 2.0 failure left scars. Any tool that feels like automation rather than assistance will be refused. _[research-summary.md — "dispatchers refused to adopt it"; customer-brief.md — "We need an assistant… not replace dispatch"]_

## Persona 2: Irena Dąbrowska _(Secondary)_

> **Grade:** A
> **Sources read:** `sources/transcript-workshop-1.md`, `sources/transcript-kickoff.md`, `engagement/cloudtest/customer-brief.md`

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Fictional name** | Irena Dąbrowska |
| **Role / Key characteristic** | Head of Customer Service Escalations — absorbs the downstream shock of every dispatcher miss |
| **High-level description** | Irena leads the team that fields angry customer calls when SLA breaches happen — or, more accurately, when customers are surprised by SLA breaches. She handles ~120 escalations per week, 80 of which are not really about the breach itself but about the lack of advance warning. She is a secondary user of the system: she doesn't dispatch technicians, but she will be the primary beneficiary of the early-warning capability because it changes what she can say to a customer before a breach becomes a complaint. |

### Key needs

- **60–90 minutes advance notice of likely SLA breaches** — enough time for her team to proactively contact the customer, softening the relationship before a breach formally occurs. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 21–22]_
- **Alert delivery in Teams, not the dispatch dashboard** — Irena's team is in Outlook, CRM, and Teams during escalation handling; they are not watching the dispatcher's work queue. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 44–48]_
- **Reduction in manual escalation volume** — fewer surprise-breach calls means her team can focus on genuine service recovery rather than information delivery. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 25–27]_
- **Credit-authorisation context** — when she does need to issue a goodwill credit, she needs the order history and breach root cause in one view to set the right amount. _[transcript-workshop-1.md line 26]_

### Key pains

- **~80 of 120 weekly escalations are preventable** — the dominant source of complaints is not the breach itself but the customer not being told in advance. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 21–27]_
- **Each escalation call costs 30–60 minutes plus credit issuance** — every call is expensive in staff time and in margin. _[transcript-workshop-1.md line 26 — "Each one costs me 30-60 minutes plus whatever credit I have to hand out."]_
- **No visibility into dispatch risk from her workflow** — Irena has no way to see which orders are likely to breach until a dispatcher calls her or a customer complains. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 44–48]_
- **Reactive rather than proactive customer service posture** — the current system forces her team to respond to problems rather than prevent them; this erodes customer trust over time. _[customer-brief.md — "We find out we breached when the customer phones us."]_

### Quote(s)

> "About 70% of the complaints I handle aren't about the breach itself, they're about being surprised by the breach. If we could give customers 60-90 minutes of warning we'd cut my escalation queue in half."
>
> — Anya Petrov (Irena's source persona), `sources/transcript-workshop-1.md` lines 21–22

> "I deal with about 120 escalations a week. Of those, maybe 80 are 'you didn't tell me.' If we eliminated half of those, that's 40 fewer angry customer calls per week. Each one costs me 30-60 minutes plus whatever credit I have to hand out."
>
> — Anya Petrov (Irena's source persona), `sources/transcript-workshop-1.md` lines 25–26

### User context / device info

- **Primary device(s)**: Desktop + phone; dual-screen setup with Outlook and CRM open simultaneously
- **Where they work**: Office environment (likely Warsaw or a regional hub — not confirmed in sources); phone-heavy workflow during escalation hours
- **Not in the dispatch system**: Irena's team does not use the dispatcher UI and should not be expected to check it. Teams notifications are the appropriate delivery channel for SLA risk alerts. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 44–48]_
- **Time pressure**: Escalations are live situations — customers are already on the phone when alerts arrive. 60-90 minutes warning is the threshold below which proactive communication becomes impossible. _[transcript-workshop-1.md lines 21–22]_
